## Description

A couple stands in a quiet room, hand in hand. Nothing here is accidental: every object seems to be whispering something.

## Story

Imagine opening a door in 1434 and finding this couple in the middle of a hushed moment. He raises his right hand, as if taking an oath or greeting someone who has just walked in. She rests her hand in his, head tilted, eyes lowered. Nobody speaks.

What is happening? For a long time scholars read it as a wedding held at home, with Van Eyck's signature on the wall acting like a witness's signature, and the two tiny figures in the mirror as the other witnesses. Others now think it may simply be a portrait of a married couple, not a ceremony at all. Nobody can say for sure, and that uncertainty is exactly what keeps people staring. (And her round belly? Probably not a pregnancy: the fashion was to gather yards of heavy fabric in front.)

Look at their faces. Neither smiles. He seems formal and a little guarded, she calm and patient. Perhaps they are thinking about the future: a household, a family, a business. As a viewer you feel like a guest who walked in a moment too early. You are seeing something private, and the mirror reminds you that someone else stands in the room too, and that someone is you.

## Look closer

- The round mirror on the back wall shows the whole room from behind, including two people standing in the doorway.
- The frame of that mirror holds ten tiny scenes from the story of Christ's Passion.
- Above the mirror, the painter signed the wall: 'Jan van Eyck was here, 1434'.

## Context

Bruges in the 1430s was one of the richest cities in Europe, a hub of cloth, banking and trade where merchants from Italy settled. The man is usually identified as Giovanni di Nicolao Arnolfini, an Italian merchant living there, though historians disagree about which member of the family it is. Everything in the room says money: the imported oranges on the windowsill, the carpet, the brass chandelier, the fur-lined clothes.

The painting is also a showcase for a new technique. Instead of fast-drying tempera, Van Eyck used oil paint in thin, transparent layers, which let him capture the glow of a window, the sheen of silk and the softness of fur. This is why every surface looks so real. The panel entered the National Gallery in London in 1842, where it still hangs.

## Artist

Jan van Eyck (c. 1390–1441) was among the most celebrated painters of his age. He worked as a court painter and trusted servant of Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, and even travelled on diplomatic missions for him, including a trip to Portugal to paint a future bride for the duke. A popular old myth says he invented oil painting. He did not, but he took it to a level of detail and glow that astonished everyone.

## When this was painted

In 1434 Van Eyck was in his forties and had been settled in Bruges for a few years, working for the duke while accepting private commissions from wealthy locals. Signing a work with a playful sentence like 'was here', as if he were one of the guests, was highly unusual for the time. It also tells us the exact year it was painted.
